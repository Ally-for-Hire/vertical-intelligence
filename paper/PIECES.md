## 0. The user's baseline assumptions (givens), and what the evidence says so far

| # | Assumption (user) | Evidence that bears on it | Assessment | How to test it here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Human brains are only as smart as evolution needed them to be | The human brain has the neurons expected of a primate its size; the cortex holds only 19% of the neurons; humans stand out by size, not by a special design (Herculano-Houzel 2009, *Front. Hum. Neurosci.*) [v]. Parrots and songbirds pack primate-like neuron numbers into walnut-sized brains, a different design reaching similar cognition (Olkowicz et al. 2016, *PNAS*) [v]. Cognition is well described as optimal use of limited resources (Lieder & Griffiths 2020, *BBS*) [v]. A large share of brain energy goes to signalling, which favours codes with at most ~15% of neurons active (Attwell & Laughlin 2001, *JCBFM*; the action-potential cost estimates were later revised downward, Howarth et al. 2012) [v] | **Consistent with the evidence.** Brains look like budget-limited solutions: many designs work, and each sits where the energy and wiring budget allows. The open question is which features are *computational principles* and which are *budget patches* | Remove each constraint and see whether learning gets better or worse. If removing energy limits makes learning *worse*, the constraint is doing useful work (a regulariser), not just a cost. NeuroBlock found a natural energy economy ~36x cheaper at equal accuracy, but did not test generalisation |
| A2 | The perfect thinking machine rests on a fundamental understanding we don't have yet | Candidate unifying principles exist but none is established: free-energy / prediction-error minimisation (Friston 2010, *Nat. Rev. Neurosci.*, which itself says its unifying power "has yet to be established") [v]; prediction as compression, MDL (Rissanen 1978) [v-prior]; intelligence as skill-acquisition efficiency given priors and experience (Chollet 2019, arXiv) [v]. Counterpoint: over 70 years, general methods that scale with computation (search, learning) beat built-in human knowledge (Sutton 2019, "The Bitter Lesson") [v] | **Open, and testable only indirectly.** The Bitter Lesson is the strongest argument *against* copying brain details. Its own advice ("build in only the meta-methods that discover complexity") is close to the user's "learning to learn" | Treat each candidate principle as a hypothesis that predicts which pieces matter. A principle earns its place if its predictions about the pieces pass |
| A3 | The start will be a perfect human brain scan | Whole-brain wiring exists for the fly (FlyWire 2024) [v-prior]. A cubic millimetre of mouse visual cortex has wiring plus the recorded activity of ~75,000 neurons, ~200,000 cells and ~0.5 billion synapses; neurons with similar responses connect more (MICrONS 2025, *Nature* 640) [v]. A cubic millimetre of human cortex: ~57,000 cells, ~150 million synapses, 1.4 petabytes; glia outnumber neurons 2:1 (Shapson-Coe et al. 2024, *Science*) [v]. But a wiring diagram is "necessary but not sufficient" (Bargmann & Marder 2013, *Nat. Methods*; the phrase is attributed to it by later work) [v]. A single cortical neuron behaves like a 5-8-layer deep network once dendritic NMDA channels are included (Beniaguev et al. 2021, *Neuron*) [v]. Our own fly test: a learner cannot recover wiring from activity when most neurons are hidden (C23) | **Plausible as a starting point, not as a sufficient one.** A scan must capture more than who connects to whom: synapse strength, dendritic detail, neuromodulator systems, and glia. Electron microscopy does not show neuromodulator state or learning rules | Test what a scan *must* contain: simulate worlds wired from FlyWire with and without each ingredient (signs, weights, dendritic non-linearity, neuromodulation) and measure how much behaviour is lost when each is missing |

## 1. The pieces

Categories: **B** = brain system or mechanism, **P** = psychology / development, **T** = theory of intelligence, **AI** = what modern AI already borrowed. "Foundation" links to the user's own givens (`THE_DREAM.md`, NeuroBlock `FOUNDATIONS.md`). "Here" = what this project has already tested.

## 2. Which pieces to test first (proposed ranking)

This ranking uses three things: how directly a piece serves the user's foundations, whether it can be tested cleanly on our small worlds, and how much evidence suggests it matters.

1. **B1 fast + slow memory with replay.** Strong evidence, directly serves "store little, reuse, connect every problem", easy to test on the existing lifelong sequence.
2. **B7 neuromodulated learning rate.** Serves the user's sliding learning rate and "recognise the change immediately"; clean test on regime-change worlds.
3. **P5 mastery / chunking.** Serves "repeating costs zero"; measurable as cost per repeat.
4. **B9 sleep at equal compute.** The user's parked sleep study, now with a fair control.
5. **P3 curiosity as learning progress.** Tests why curiosity failed earlier (C3): the worlds had no unlearnable noise source.
6. **B11 energy as a regulariser (tests A1).** Does an energy budget help generalisation, or only cost?
7. **P6 Self v0 with on-demand parts.** The user's main focus; needs B1, B7 and P3 first.
8. **B14 / A3 scan ingredients.** What a brain scan must contain, using FlyWire and MICrONS-style wiring.
9. **T3 library learning in a rune universe.** The direct test of "foundational blocks" and "all problems relate".
10. The rest (B3, B5, B6, B8, B10, B12, B13, P1, P4) as the earlier tests open them up.

The existing RIGOR items (the gate's variance, mechanism, constants) continue as the rigor treatment of piece B4 (learning to learn). They are no longer the main line.

**Cross-piece patterns so far:** (1) several mechanisms only work as PAIRS (T7: shrink + slow; B7: reset + boost); (2) the hardest single problem is knowing WHICH situation you are in (mastery, parts, the Self), and when unsure the right move is to check, not to average; (3) structure is worth exactly as much as it is shared (T3 dose-response; transfer literature); (4) 'few connections matter' wins in three separate pieces: the wiring budget (B11), the focused forward model (B5) and sparse dendritic branches (B10), echoing very sparse real wiring (C22); (5) curiosity's proven job is avoiding the unlearnable; choosing well among the learnable needs motivation (persist on the hardest learnable thing), and only the two together beat random (P4, replicated).
