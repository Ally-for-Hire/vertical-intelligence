Curious-net

A one-day research sprint on machines that learn what affects what and know when they might be wrong.
October 22, 2026 · run with AI research assistants, ideas and direction by the project owner

The dream: a large language model that can discover quantum physics on its own. It is given only the science people knew before quantum theory, plus the measurements that puzzled them around 1900 (the colours of light from hot objects, the sharp colours of hydrogen, light knocking electrons out of metal). It is never told quantum theory exists. It passes if it proposes that energy must come in discrete chunks. More broadly: a language model that can actually learn and create semi-new things.

We did not try to build that mind. Instead we broke the dream into small, testable claims and ran experiments on small simulated worlds, plus some real brain-wiring data from the fruit fly. For each claim we asked one thing: does this actually hold up, and what would a skeptic say? Below is what we found, including the surprises and the places where we were wrong.

How to read this. The main text is meant to be read straight through without any math. The gold "Deep dive" boxes open up the actual mathematics behind each result for anyone curious. They are optional.

Everything here is early-stage and was done in one day on one computer. Nothing has been independently replicated yet. The worlds are small simulations, not the real world (except for the fly wiring data). The value is in the shape of the findings and in how they connect to real published research.

2. How the research direction evolved during the day

  1. Morning: a curious on/off neuron network learning to drive a toy car around a track, with "sleep" cycles to consolidate memories. Sleep was later set aside as a separate future study, so its effect could be isolated.
  2. A continuous lifelong learner that should recognise an old problem with one change, e.g. "same car, more drag," immediately.
  3. A "self-learning neural network": an ordinary network plus a small add-on that learns, across problems, which kinds of inputs tend to matter. This became the main line.
  4. The long-range vision was written down: a self-improving, curious, skeptical language model, an offline AI parent teaching it from zero, ties to the fly brain, and a future three-part brain design (sensors, emotion, a central "Self").
  5. Mid-day: a fast research loop. Each round tested one claim, updated a "claims ledger," and checked the published literature.
  6. An independent review by a second, stronger AI read all the code and results and found real errors (see "Shake-ups").
  7. Afternoon redirect: the teacher is an oracle of objective truth. The student must run its own experiments, with perfect instruments by default, and should learn from real data, poke holes in bad theories and derive laws.

3. Results: what held up

Knowing what matters is worth a lot, but only when there's clutter

With a handful of inputs, telling a network which ones matter makes no difference. With 200 useless inputs mixed in, it roughly halves the network's early mistakes (error 0.84 → 0.40).

Deep dive: why clutter hurts a learner

A network has to learn one or more weights per input, and every weight it learns from noisy data carries a bit of error. A useful rule of thumb from statistics:

extra error ≈ (number of weights being learned) ÷ (number of examples seen)

So 200 useless inputs mean hundreds of weights that can only add error, and the learner needs far more examples before it settles. If you knew that only, say, 3 of the 200 inputs matter, you'd only pay for 3. Even searching for which few matter costs only about s·log(p) examples' worth (s = true inputs, p = all inputs) instead of p. That logarithm is the whole value of "knowing what affects what": it turns a cost that grows with the clutter into one that barely notices it.

Learning from past problems helps, but less than hoped, and it stops improving

After about 10 earlier problems, the learned add-on recovers a real share of that benefit. Then it plateaus: more experience doesn't help with this design. That matches theory. A learner that has learned a family of problems has learned all there is to learn about that family.

Deep dive: what "learning from experience" really learns, and why it plateaus

In probability terms, experience across problems builds a prior, the learner's expectations before it sees the new problem. Bayes' rule combines that with the new evidence:

belief after evidence ∝ (what I expected before) × (how well the evidence fits)

Each past problem sharpens the "what I expected" part, for example "inputs that wiggle in step with the output usually matter; slow drifting ones usually don't." But once the prior matches how this family of problems is actually generated, there is nothing more to learn from more of the same. The plateau is a mathematical ceiling, not a bug. To keep improving, the learner needs genuinely new kinds of problems.

A second finding explains how the add-on helps. It multiplies each input by a number p between 0 and 1. In the optimiser these networks use, that makes the input's weight learn about p times slower. So the add-on is really a learned learning-speed per input: suspicious inputs aren't thrown away, just learned slowly. That is why mistakes in its judgement are cheap.

Self-improvement across 150 generations did not collapse

A big worry in AI research is "model collapse": systems trained on their own output slowly degrade (Shumailov et al., Nature 2024). Here, 150 generations improved and then held steady. The reason is understood: every generation learned from fresh real data and carried forward only a tiny summary.

Deep dive: why training on your own output can't create knowledge

Information theory has a result called the data-processing inequality. No amount of processing of a signal can increase the information it contains about the world:

information(world ; my output) ≤ information(world ; what I observed)

A model trained only on its own outputs is processing a copy of a copy, so errors and blind spots compound. That is model collapse. Our generations avoided it because each one looked at new real data (new information enters every time), and only a tiny "what tends to matter" summary was handed down. A self-improving mind therefore needs a continuing source of fresh evidence: experiments, observations or a trustworthy teacher.

Law discovery with perfect instruments is easy, if the law is a simple one

A simulated student chose its own drop heights and timed each fall exactly. With just five drops it identified the right law every time. It noticed every time the world followed a law it didn't know, with no false alarms, and caught claims that were wrong by as little as 1%. It rejected the "15 m in 5 s on Earth" claim, and noted that it would be true on a world with gravity 1.2 m/s² (about the Moon's 1.62). With 3% measurement noise, small errors slip through again.

Deep dive: how the student picks a law and catches a bad claim

Candidate laws. The student knows three: free fall, t = √(2h/g); constant speed, t = h/v; and fall with air drag. It fits each to its drops and scores them with the Bayesian Information Criterion:

score = (how badly the law misses the data) + (number of adjustable knobs) × log(number of drops)

The second term is Occam's razor in numbers: a law with more knobs must earn them by fitting much better.

Checking "15 m in 5 s". Free fall gives g = 2h/t² = 2·15/25 = 1.2 m/s². On Earth (9.8 m/s²) the fall takes about 1.75 s, so the claim is off by a factor of almost 3 and is rejected every time. The student can also say what would make it true: a world with gravity about an eighth of Earth's.

Why perfect instruments change everything. With noise of size σ, the smallest deviation from a law you can detect after n measurements shrinks only like σ/√n. Halving it costs four times the experiments. With σ = 0, any real deviation shows up immediately, so the hard part moves from statistics to imagination: having the right law among your candidates.

The real fly brain

We analysed the public FlyWire whole-brain map: 138,639 neurons and 54.5 million synapses. Real wiring is very sparse. Half of a typical neuron's input comes from just 3 partners, and 90% from about 9. Our simulated worlds turned out to match the dominant connections but not the long tail.

Deep dive: what "sparse" and "heavy-tailed" mean here

Counting a connection when two neurons share at least 5 synapses (the field's convention), a typical fly neuron listens to about 9 others (median), out of 138,639 possible. That is about 0.007% of the brain. "Heavy-tailed" means a few neurons are enormous hubs: the top 1% of neurons receive 21% of all incoming synapses, and one receives from over 5,000 partners. Weighting by synapse count, half of a neuron's input comes from about 3 partners. So "a few strong causes plus a long tail of weak ones" is a realistic picture of what affects what in a real brain.

4. Results: what did not hold up (and why that's useful)

BeliefVerdictWhat happened
Curiosity beats random explorationnot in simple worldsWhen effects are immediate and independent, random experiments are already nearly ideal. With perfect instruments, clever and random drop heights did equally well.
Quantum-style states helponly in a nicheThey win only when the data really has quantum-like structure, like question-order effects in surveys, and then only slightly. On ordinary data they cost accuracy.
Emotion as structured randomness finds deeper linksnot supportedA population of randomised variants never beat the single best learner. Randomness only helped as insurance when the learner was confidently wrong.
Neural networks can't do thisrefutedThe published literature shows ordinary networks trained across many tasks already learn this way, and our own add-on is itself a small neural network.
The add-on learns cause and effectnoIt learns "what is useful to predict with," not "what causes what." Taught the true causes, it did slightly worse. On fly-wiring worlds it couldn't recover the wiring at all, which is a known hard problem in neuroscience.
Deep dive: why "useful for predicting" is not "causes"

Suppose A causes B, and C is just a noisy copy of A. Then C predicts B almost as well as A does, but changing C does nothing to B. From watching alone, the two can be statistically indistinguishable. With hidden variables in the system (like 250 unobserved neurons in the fly-wiring worlds), the problem gets worse: true direct connections can look weak, and indirect ones strong. Neuroscience has proven versions of this for recurrent networks. The way out is intervention: nudge A yourself and see whether B moves. In causal-inference notation, the question changes from P(B | saw A) to P(B | did A). That is why the project now insists the student runs its own experiments.

Deep dive: why the quantum idea helps only in a niche

A quantum bit is described by amplitudes whose squares are probabilities. If the amplitudes are ordinary real numbers and you only ever ask one question, that is just a probability written differently, so there is nothing to gain. The difference appears when questions are asked in sequence and the order matters. Quantum rules then force a specific balance, the "quantum question equality":

P(yes,no) + P(no,yes) asked A-then-B  =  the same sum asked B-then-A

Real survey data appear to obey this (Wang et al., PNAS 2014). On data with this structure, a quantum-style model needs fewer adjustable knobs and wins slightly. On ordinary data it is the wrong shape and loses.

5. Shake-ups: where we caught ourselves being wrong

  • "Most of the gain is experience" turned out to be mostly not experience. A simple statistic, softly weighted with just two tuned dials, gets about 70% of the learned add-on's benefit. Experience across problems adds the remaining ~30%. That part is real, but modest.
  • A "curiosity wins" result was an artefact. At small budgets the "curious" strategy was secretly choosing randomly. In the fair re-test it helps a little, but only when measurements are noisy.
  • A yardstick was wrong. Results were quoted as "% of the best possible," but the "best possible" reference was beaten in two settings, so it wasn't the best possible.
  • The simulated worlds changed mid-day. A code change silently made "world #9000" a different world in later experiments. This explained why two runs of the same test disagreed.
  • A theory note was mathematically wrong about a trust statistic staying stable. It doesn't: it drifts, growing like the square root of time.
  • The "no arbitrary numbers" rule was broken. The project's audit, meant to ensure every unjustified number is flagged, found 76 unregistered numbers in the newer code.

6. The newest result: a student taught only the truth

We taught the student the exact truth on 100 practice worlds, then asked it "how likely is this input a cause?" on new worlds.

  • On familiar worlds its confidence is honest. When it says 2%, about 2% really are causes.
  • On worlds built differently, it is confidently wrong. On fly-wiring worlds it said about 1% while the truth was about 24%.
  • It can't feel that it is wrong. Asking several copies of it whether they disagree, the standard self-doubt test, backfired: they agreed more exactly where they were wrong.
  • "This looks unlike what I was taught" mostly works. Comparing the new world's raw data with the practice data flagged most of the dangerous cases.
Deep dive: honest confidence, and why self-doubt failed

Honest confidence ("calibration") has a simple test. Gather every case where the system said "70% sure" and check that it was right about 70% of the time, and the same for every other confidence level. Our taught student passed this on familiar worlds: the average gap was under 1 percentage point.

Why the copies agreed when wrong. All five copies were trained on the same lessons, so they share the same blind spot. On an unfamiliar world each one confidently gives the same wrong answer. Agreement measures how well the lessons pinned the answer down, not whether the lessons apply. Comparing the raw data of the new world with the practice data asks a different question, "have I seen anything like this?", and that worked much better.

A statistical safety net that misled. A method called conformal prediction promises "the truth is in my answer set 90% of the time." It kept that promise by answering "not a cause" almost always. Real causes were only about 2% of cases, so that answer is right 98% of the time while missing nearly every real cause. Averages can hide exactly the cases you care about.

Why this matters for the vision: being taught the truth gives honest confidence only inside the territory that was taught. Healthy skepticism needs two more things: a sense of "am I in familiar territory?" that comes from the data, not from the model's own feelings of certainty, and the ability to run an experiment to check. Both are now central to the project.

9. Limits of the math worth knowing

  • Watching isn't enough to find causes. Without experiments, something that merely tracks a cause looks the same as the cause.
  • Experience has a ceiling. A learner can't beat the information in what it looks at. Within one family of problems, learning-to-learn stops improving once the family is learned. "Faster forever" needs ever-new kinds of problems.
  • No universal "I'm out of my depth" detector exists. Confidence is only trustworthy relative to what the system was trained on. The realistic goal is honest inside its territory, abstaining at a detectable boundary, and able to run experiments to check.
  • A system trained only on its own output gains no new information about the world. Fresh real data is essential.
  • Perfect teacher versus skepticism. A perfect teacher makes the student certain, and certainty leaves no room for doubt. How the oracle should teach its own limits is an open research question in this project.

10. What's next

  • Students that run experiments in many-variable worlds (nudging one input and watching what changes), where choosing the right experiment should matter.
  • Inventing the missing law once the student knows none of its laws fit.
  • Three ways for the oracle to teach its limits: facts only; facts plus a stated scope; or showing data near and beyond where the law breaks down.
  • Real data: derive Kepler's third law from actual planetary orbits, and reject bad theories with it.
