# Dialogue 1: a summary of the author's statements

Read this if: you want to know where theorems U6 to U18 and the question Q10 came from.

Over a long working session (26 to 27 October 2026) the author deliberately argued with an AI
assistant to sharpen their own thinking: first whether predicting the next word can be intelligence,
then, thinking aloud, what else a mind needs. The verbatim transcript contains personal reflections
and is kept in the author's private notes (the author's decision, 2026-10-27). This page summarizes
it. The claims are the author's; where something personal led to a claim it is described only at the
level needed to show what led to it.

## What the author claimed, and what led to it

| Id | The author's claim | What led to it |
|---|---|---|
| U6 | Intelligence is the ability to use randomness to your best benefit; a fixed, trained predictor cannot make good randomness or its own ideas | The author's view that evolution starts from randomness, set against how a language model is trained (a frozen dataset, then gradient descent) |
| U7 | The parts of a mind have intrinsic weights and can take over; the aim is a Self that holds all of them | The author's knowledge of internal family systems (a therapy model of the mind as parts) and of Minsky's society of mind |
| U8 | Simulation inside the mind, and the drive to improve that simulation, are foundational | An ability the author sees as beyond prediction: building and working in an imagined scene |
| U9 | Boredom is the want to learn plus the inability to learn; the need to learn is a requirement, not a choice | The author's own experience of the difference between chosen stillness (meditation) and forced inactivity |
| U10 | Two needs compete, to be more and to be all of yourself; being all of yourself comes first, and wholeness multiplies growth | Attachment research (children stop exploring when unsafe) and the author's observations of their own learning |
| U11 | A part frozen by early experience is an ungrown snapshot; it must be fully expressed before it can learn | The internal-family-systems idea that unprocessed early experience stays unchanged until it is revisited; the author treats it as a model, not a clinical claim |
| U12 | The drive is not uncertainty reduction; certainty past a point is harmful | The author's own experience that being certain and idle is unpleasant, and the dark-room problem in neuroscience |
| U13 | Some aspect of a biological substrate is part of the solution; the missing link is in the representation of knowledge, not the knowledge | The author's intuition, then the finding that excitatory/inhibitory structure mattered most in the project's own wiring experiments |
| U14 | Time is not required for intelligence | The author's observation that slow thinkers can be highly intelligent |
| U15 | Genius is drive that keeps a problem alive past the point where conscious work stops, and the drive is mostly learned | The author's description of staying with a problem until the answer arrives; a recent personal experience of suddenly seeing a problem differently, which the author links to a period of felt safety |
| U16 | Intuition is the warning that part of yourself is being ignored; it grows if ignored, dims but never stops | The author's description of a persistent sense that something is wrong when a need is pushed aside |
| U17 | Comfort-seeking and growth are one hunger, grown from one ancient "better or worse for me" signal | The author's intuition, then the bacterium example (one signal steering both approach and avoidance) |
| U18 | Gradient descent is not the enemy; it is probably the fastest learner and should be intrinsic to everything, and what is vital is something that manages it | The author's reflection on what the earlier experiments showed |
| Q10 | What is the "for me", the self that makes anything better or worse for it? This is the path forward | The end of the session: the author judged that "better or worse" is mostly understood and "for me" is the deeper question |
